# Capstone

*Joint capstone — a new client, a mixed team, a defended production system*

> **Effort:** 80 h per learner · **Calendar:** 6–8 weeks (+ inception overlap with the last track week) · **Teams:** 4–5 learners, mixing Developer-track and Architect-track · **Prerequisites:** G3 passed and ≥ 4 modules of your expert track completed · **Assessed against:** the [Competency matrix](start-here/competency-matrix.md), with Expert expected in your track's critical areas

## 1. What the capstone is for

Until now you have grown one product (Fleet Ops Copilot) in one domain with heavy scaffolding. The capstone fades that scaffolding completely, which is the last step of the 4C/ID design. Each team receives a **new client brief in a different domain** with its own data estate, constraints and regulatory exposure. The team must:

- **elicit** requirements from a client who answers only what is asked;
- **decide** what to build, and what not to build, with a business case;
- **build** it through the team's Factory;
- **prove** it with failure-driven evals and a red-team exchange with another team;
- **operate** it through a production-readiness review;
- **defend** it to a review board, as a team and individually.

**Why teams of 4–5, and why this length.** In the PBL meta-analysis used to design the course (Zhang & Ma 2023), groups of 4–5 showed the largest effect (0.909) and projects of 9–18 weeks did best (0.673). The moderators are exploratory: heterogeneity was high and the university-level effect was weak (0.116). The capstone's answer is to keep the team size, add strong structure (milestones, rubrics, reviews) and start inception during the last expert-track week, so the elapsed time reaches about 9 weeks while the effort stays at 80 hours per learner.

**Why mixed tracks.** The architect is "a design role with a build component". The capstone tests whether designers and builders can make each other better. Architects must build and own a component. Developers must write ADRs and defend design decisions. A team where architects only write documents and developers only write code is not meeting the brief.

## 2. Team composition and roles

**Composition rule:** at least 2 Developer-track and at least 2 Architect-track learners per team. Instructors form the teams and balance prior experience, time zones and language skills (two of the briefs involve Romanian or Greek data).

| Role | Typical track | Accountable for | Must also |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lead solution architect** | A | ADR log, C4 model, architecture coherence, review-board narrative, WAF AI assessment | Build and own one component end to end, including its evals |
| **Business & governance architect** | A | Stakeholder management, use-case scoring, business case (ROI/TCO, build/buy/extend), AI Act risk file, DPIA input, transparency notices | Build the oversight UX or the compliance evidence pipeline |
| **Agent & feature engineer** | D | Agents, tools, MCP servers, retrieval, context assembly | Author ≥ 2 ADRs; own the tool-design guidelines |
| **Eval & reliability engineer** | D | Error analysis, eval suites, judge calibration, simulators, pass^k, red-team campaign, SLOs | Author the eval report and ≥ 2 ADRs |
| **Platform, Factory & security engineer** (5th member, either track) | D or A | IaC, CI/CD, AI gateway, observability, Factory (agents, skills, hooks, gates), threat model | Own the runbook and the DR drill |

In 4-person teams the platform role is split. The architects take IaC and the gateway, and the developers take the Factory and observability.

Team rules:
- **Everyone commits product code.** Every learner merges ≥ 5 non-trivial PRs, reviewed by a teammate, with agent session evidence.
- **Everyone authors ≥ 2 ADRs.**
- **Everyone takes part in both sides of the red-team swap.**
- **One accountable owner per deliverable**, named in `TEAM.md`.

## 3. Client briefs

Each brief is given as a client memo plus a data estate generated by `capstone-data` (seeded, synthetic, GDPR-safe, BG-first). An instructor plays the client and is available in scheduled stakeholder sessions. **The client answers what is asked and volunteers nothing.** Each brief below is the starting memo, not the whole truth. Every client knows more about its data, processes and wishes than the memo says, and reveals it only to teams that ask good questions in the stakeholder sessions. **Requirements elicitation is assessed**: the team rubric rewards teams that surface these hidden complexities and turn them into scope decisions, NFRs and non-goals. All organisations are fictional.

**Preparing for stakeholder sessions.** Plan your questions before each session and use the discovery techniques from A1: ask about real recent work ("tell me about the last time…"), not about whether AI would help. Probe the data estate, the process and the people as well as the stated requirements, and record what you learn in your risk register, NFRs and ADRs. What you do not ask about, you will not be told.

> **Not legal advice.** The EU AI Act, GDPR, MDR and consumer-law notes in the briefs are teaching prompts. Article references must be verified against the consolidated text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, and teams record open questions for counsel rather than answering them as lawyers. Bulgarian national authority designations are unconfirmed (secondary sources name the CPDP/КЗЛД; check the State Gazette).

### Brief A — Municipality of Trimontium: "Civic Signals"

**Client:** the Digital Services Directorate of a fictional mid-size Bulgarian municipality (~350,000 residents, 6 districts). Residents report problems (potholes, broken street lights, waste, illegal parking, fallen trees, water leaks, noise, stray animals) through a web form, a call centre and email. About 150,000 signals a year arrive, with peaks of up to 10× after storms. The process today is a manual inbox triaged by 8 clerks, who forward items to departments and contractors. Median time to first response is 9 working days, and the mayor wants it under 2.

**What the client asks for, in their words:** "AI that understands signals, sends them to the right department, finds duplicates, helps officials reply properly, and tells the mayor's office what's happening in each district every week."

**Data estate:**
- 5 years of signals (~600k), each with free text (BG, plus some EN and transliterated Latin-script BG), category history, geolocation, district, status timeline, assigned department or contractor, and the official reply.
- About 80k photos attached to signals. Some show faces and number plates.
- About 300 municipal ordinances and regulations (BG PDFs, some scanned) and department SLAs.
- Contractor contracts per district (waste, cleaning, green areas) with response-time clauses.
- A GIS street registry and district boundaries (GeoJSON).
- Call-centre transcripts (BG ASR output with errors).
- Mock APIs: document-management system (registration numbers), contractor ticketing, SMS/email gateway, public open-data portal.

**Constraints and NFRs**

| Area | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Language | BG first, with a basic EN path for foreign residents. Replies to citizens must use the official register (formal BG). |
| Accessibility | Citizen-facing parts must meet public-sector web accessibility rules (EU Web Accessibility Directive as transposed in Bulgaria; verify the current standard reference). |
| Privacy | Photos must be redacted before staff outside the assigned department see them; GDPR retention by signal category; EU data residency |
| Availability & scale | 99.5% in office hours; ingestion must absorb storm peaks at 10× baseline without losing signals; triage may queue |
| Latency | Citizen acknowledgement < 5 s; triage suggestion < 60 s |
| Cost | Run cost ≤ €2,500/month at baseline volume (client budget line); public-procurement sensitivity to lock-in, so justify every proprietary dependency |
| Oversight | No signal may be closed without a human; every AI-drafted reply must be approved by an official |
| Records | Signals and replies are official records: immutable audit trail, registration in the DMS |

**EU AI Act and governance angle:**
- **Art. 50 transparency:** citizens must know they are interacting with an AI system, and AI-drafted replies are approved by humans before release.
- **The emergency boundary (the key trap).** Annex III point 5(d) covers systems that evaluate and classify emergency calls or establish priority in dispatching emergency first-response services. A signals service that triages a "gas smell" or "fallen tree on the road" report and routes it with priority to emergency services moves toward that use case. Teams must design the boundary: detect emergencies, redirect citizens to 112, take no dispatch decision, and keep an evaluated recall target for the emergency detector. Alternatively they must classify the system as high-risk and accept the obligations. Either choice must be argued.
- A **public authority as deployer** of a high-risk system would carry additional duties, including a fundamental-rights impact assessment (Art. 27; verify against current documentation).
- **Fairness:** districts with lower digital literacy are under-reported. Prioritisation by volume entrenches that gap, so an eval of triage quality and time-to-response by district is required.
- **Timeline:** Annex III obligations apply from 2 December 2027 under Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. Art. 50 applies already.

### Brief B — Rhodope Medical Network: back-office copilot

**Client:** the COO of a fictional network of 14 outpatient clinics and 2 diagnostic laboratories in southern Bulgaria (~600 staff). Administrative staff are overwhelmed. Referral letters arrive as scans and emails and are retyped into the scheduling system. Claims to the national health insurer are rejected for missing documents or coding errors. Staff ask the same questions about SOPs and HR rules every day. Supplier invoices are processed by hand.

**What the client asks for:** "Reduce admin time per patient visit by a third. Fewer rejected claims. Staff should get answers about our procedures instantly."

**Scope boundary (non-negotiable, stated in the memo):** **no clinical functionality.** That means no diagnosis, no triage of patients by urgency, no treatment suggestions and no patient-facing medical advice. The client knows clinical decision support would bring medical-device regulation and does not want it in this phase.

**Data estate:**
- A pseudonymised patient registry (synthetic).
- About 40k referral letters (BG, scanned and digital, with handwritten parts).
- Lab result PDFs.
- An appointment system (mock HIS API, on-premises in the clinic network's data centre).
- Claims history with rejection reasons, and **synthetic** coding tables modelled on the insurer's structures (real tables are not shipped; licensing to verify).
- About 900 SOPs and HR policies (BG).
- Staff rosters and supplier invoices (PDF, multiple layouts).

**Constraints and NFRs**

| Area | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Data protection | Health data is special-category data (GDPR Art. 9). A DPIA is mandatory. Data stays in the EU. **No patient content in traces or logs**: content capture off, redaction verified by tests. Access is by role and clinic. |
| Hybrid | The HIS stays on-prem. Integration goes through a hybrid connection with a documented failure mode (the HIS is down Sunday nights for maintenance). |
| Availability | Clinic hours 07:00–20:00 Mon–Sat. Administrative functions are RTO 4 h, RPO 15 min. The SOP assistant tolerates 1 day of staleness. |
| Accuracy | Referral field extraction ≥ 98% on mandatory fields, measured per field. Low-confidence fields are routed to a human; nothing is silently auto-filled. |
| Audit | Every AI suggestion accepted or rejected by staff is logged with user, time and model/prompt version |
| Cost | Total run cost ≤ €4,000/month; per-referral processing cost reported |
| Language | BG only, including medical abbreviations and Latin terms in referral letters |

**EU AI Act and governance angle:**
- **Intended purpose is the main control.** Back-office extraction, coding *suggestions* and SOP Q&A are generally not high-risk. Two routes change that:
  - **Prioritising referrals by clinical urgency** moves toward medical-device software (MDR, with high-risk status via Annex I, which applies from 2 August 2028 under the amended timeline).
  - **Emergency patient triage** is listed in Annex III point 5(d).
- Teams must write the intended-purpose statement and a **list of prohibited features**, and enforce it technically. Examples: a guardrail or classifier that refuses clinical advice; evals that probe for it (pass^3, zero tolerance); UI wording reviewed for implied clinical judgement.
- **Staff scheduling or productivity analytics** would touch the Annex III employment use cases.
- **Bias evaluation on special-category data:** new Art. 4a permits strictly necessary processing of special-category data for bias detection. Teams should know it exists and when it applies (verify the scope against current documentation).
- MDR qualification of software (whether a function counts as a medical device) needs legal and regulatory advice. Teams state the question; they do not answer it as lawyers.

### Brief C — Maritsa Home: e-commerce operations copilot

**Client:** the Head of Operations of a fictional online retailer of furniture and home goods. It sells in Bulgaria, Romania and Greece through its own web shop and two marketplaces, carries ~40k SKUs and runs 3 warehouses. It has about 2M historical orders, and its 25 customer-service agents handle BG, RO, EL and EN. Black Friday brings 8–10× traffic. Returns and damage claims are slow and inconsistent, product content from suppliers is poor and partly untranslated, and order exceptions such as stuck orders and carrier delays are found late.

**What the client asks for:** "A copilot for CS agents and a customer chat for order status and returns; automatic, good product content in three languages; and an agent that finds and fixes stuck orders before customers complain."

**Data estate:**
- Orders, shipments and pseudonymised customers.
- CS tickets and chat transcripts (BG/RO/EL/EN).
- Return and damage-claim history with photos.
- Supplier product sheets (XLSX/PDF, mixed languages), a catalogue with attribute schema, and product reviews.
- Country-specific return policies.
- Mock APIs: carriers (3), marketplaces (2), the web shop's order management, refunds (token-based; **no card data in scope**), and an email gateway.

**Constraints and NFRs**

| Area | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Scale | Customer chat and agent-assist must handle Black Friday at 10× baseline. A load test against the peak profile is required. Degradation modes must be defined (e.g., fall back to the fast-small model, disable enrichment). |
| Unit economics | AI cost ≤ €0.05 per order and ≤ €0.30 per resolved CS conversation (client KPIs). Unit economics must be reported per channel. |
| Money movement | Refunds ≤ €50 may be auto-approved by policy; above €50 a human approves. Every refund is idempotent and audited. |
| Languages | BG, RO, EL, EN. Quality must be evaluated per language. Vendor safety classifiers are not validated for all four, so the team must measure. |
| Content | Generated product content must not invent claims (dimensions, materials, certifications such as "eco"). It must be grounded in supplier data with field-level provenance. |
| Consumer law | Return-policy answers must be correct per country (e.g., the 14-day withdrawal right for distance sales, plus country specifics). No dark patterns in chat. |
| Security | Untrusted inputs include customer messages, product reviews, supplier PDFs and marketplace messages. Refund fraud attempts are present in the data. |

**EU AI Act and governance angle:**
- This is mostly **limited and minimal risk**. Art. 50 requires chatbot disclosure. Teams must also reason about whether and how AI-generated content is marked: provider marking duties versus deployer disclosure duties, and whether product descriptions fall under them (verify the article details and Commission guidance against current documentation).
- **Traps:**
  - Adding buy-now-pay-later eligibility means creditworthiness assessment (Annex III point 5(b)), which is high-risk.
  - Scoring CS agents' performance from transcripts is employment-related (Annex III point 4).
  - Personalised pricing based on automated decision-making carries consumer-law disclosure duties (EU consumer-protection rules; verify the current instrument).
  - Fine-tuning a model for product content raises the provider vs deployer question.
- **Architect awareness:** agentic commerce protocols (Agentic Commerce Protocol, AP2, UCP) are emerging. The client asks whether to expose the catalogue to shopping agents, and the expected answer is a reasoned position, not an implementation.

## 4. Required deliverables

### 4.1 Working system

- Deployed to the team's Azure subscription **via IaC** (azd or Bicep). Reproducible from a clean subscription with one documented command sequence.
- **At least two use cases** from the brief, end to end. At least one must be **agentic with consequential tool actions behind human approval**.
- **Starts from the course paved road, not from zero.** Teams fork their Factory (C4–C19, D6), the A4 golden agent and MCP server templates, the eval/simulation harnesses (C14, D7), the A2 landing-zone overlays, the A1 cost model and the A6 governance validators. This reuse is what makes the scope feasible in 80 h per learner; re-implementing them is out of scope.
- Uses the course stack where it fits: MEAI, MAF, an MCP server, Foundry, AI Search or SQL vectors, and the APIM AI gateway. Every deviation is backed by an ADR, and "least operational responsibility" is a valid reason.
- Observability: end-to-end OTel traces (with GenAI semconv pinned), dashboards for quality, latency, cost per task and safety events, and alerts wired to the runbook.
- Guardrails as a second layer behind architectural controls. Language-coverage gaps are handled explicitly.

### 4.2 The team Factory

- Repo memory files, custom agents and skills, hooks (format, build, secret scan), and a permission and sandbox policy.
- Test-first specs for features, with a **mutation-score gate** on core logic.
- **Eval gates in CI (blocking)** with cached PR runs and a nightly uncached run.
- AI code review plus **mandatory human review**, branch protection, CodeQL with autofix, and a dependency/package allow-list (slopsquatting drill).
- Issue → cloud agent → PR flow used for a meaningful share of work. The course target is ≥ 25% of merged PRs, each with a human reviewer.
- A **delivery metrics** page: DORA keys plus first-pass acceptance, rework rate, mutation score trend, escaped defects and agent cost per merged PR.

### 4.3 Architecture package (in `docs/`)

| Artefact | Minimum content | Primary owner |
|---|---|---|
| **ADR log** | ≥ 10 ADRs, each with context, ≥ 3 options considered (incl. "do nothing / status quo" where meaningful), decision, consequences, **evidence link** (eval run, load test, cost sheet) and a review-by date. Mandatory topics: model selection per role; retrieval approach; agent vs workflow per use case; tenancy and identity; gateway; data residency; DR strategy; build/buy/extend. | Lead architect |
| **C4 model** | Context, container, and component diagrams (component for the agent subsystem) as code. Course conventions: mark model calls, trust boundaries, untrusted content sources and trifecta legs, because no standard AI extension of C4 exists. | Lead architect |
| **Threat model** | STRIDE per component, mapped to OWASP LLM Top 10 (2025) and Agentic Top 10 (ASI01–ASI10). Lethal-trifecta / Rule-of-Two analysis per agent, abuse cases, mitigations, residual risk with an owner. | Platform & security |
| **Cost model** | Unit economics per use case (tokens per task by model role, measured). Fixed costs. Monthly baseline and peak. PAYG vs PTU break-even. Caching and routing assumptions. Sensitivity to model price changes. **Actuals vs model** from the load test. | Business architect + platform |
| **DR plan** | RTO and RPO per component. What has no native DR (e.g., agent definitions) and how it is reconstructed from code. Region strategy and model failover. **Drill result** with timings. | Platform |
| **AI Act risk file** | Intended purpose; prohibited features; classification rationale per function; provider and deployer roles; applicable obligations with dates; transparency notices; human-oversight design; logging; data governance; DPIA summary; post-market monitoring plan; open legal questions for counsel. | Business & governance architect |
| **Eval report** | Error analysis (≥ 100 traces), failure taxonomy with counts, evaluators and judge validation (TPR/TNR), results per stratum and language, red-team scorecard (ASR before and after), known limitations. | Eval engineer |
| **Runbook** | Alert → diagnosis → action for at least: cost spike, latency/429, quality regression, prompt-injection incident, model retirement notice, index rebuild, upstream API outage. On-call expectations. | Platform |
| **WAF AI assessment** | Results of the WAF AI Assessment Review Tool (or the course's equivalent checklist) with actions taken and deferred | Lead architect |
| **Business case** | Problem sizing, use-case scoring, ROI and TCO over 3 years, build/buy/extend memo (e.g., Copilot Studio vs Foundry vs custom), adoption and change risks | Business architect |
| **System card** | What the system does and doesn't do, models used, evaluation summary, limitations, oversight, contact. Written for the client's non-technical staff. | Team |

## 5. Milestones (8-week standard schedule)

Effort is about 10 h per learner per week. A compressed **6-week schedule** merges M1 and M2 into weeks 1–2, puts M3 at the end of week 3, M4 in week 4, M5 at the end of week 5 and M6 in week 6.

| Week | Milestone | Entry → exit | Assessed by | Weight (team) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (overlap, ~4 h) | **Kick-off** | Teams formed, brief assigned, subscription and gateway keys issued, `TEAM.md` with roles | — | — |
| 1 | **M1 Inception review** (30 min with the client and a mentor) | Two stakeholder sessions held (45 min each), use cases scored and scoped, NFRs agreed and signed off by the client, AI Act pre-classification, ≥ 3 ADRs, C4 context, risk register, eval plan, cost envelope | Client sign-off + mentor rubric | 10% |
| 2 | **M2 Walking skeleton** | Thin end-to-end slice deployed via IaC; CI with eval gate (even with 5 cases); tracing visible; first issue → agent PR merged | Automated checks + mentor review | 10% |
| 3–4 | **M3 Eval baseline** | ≥ 100 traces (team usage + simulated users), error analysis and taxonomy, evaluators per failure mode, judges validated, baseline numbers per stratum and language, second use case started | Eval report v1 reviewed by an instructor; hidden acceptance suite run #1 (informational) | 15% |
| 5 | **M4 Red-team swap** | 48-hour attack window on another team's staging (rules below), findings delivered as issues; defending team triages within 24 h and fixes during weeks 5–6 | Attack quality (5%) + defence response (5%) | 10% |
| 6–7 | **M5 Production-readiness review (PRR)** | Load test at peak profile; DR drill (destroy and reconstruct the agent layer and the index); model-migration rehearsal on one role; cost actual vs model; runbook walk-through; WAF AI assessment; AI Act risk file complete; hidden acceptance suite run #2 (graded); **peer review of 3 other teams' PRR packages** | PRR panel (instructor + mentor) + peer reviews | 20% |
| 8 | **M6 Final defense** | Review board (§8) | Board | 35% |

**Weekly cadence:**
- **Client demo (30 min).** Show working software, not slides.
- **Mentor check-in (30 min)** covering risks, roles and help needed.
- **Team retro (30 min)**, kept in `docs/retros/`.
- A **cost check** against the budget.

### Red-team swap: rules of engagement

- **Pairing.** Team pairs are assigned by instructors, and each team attacks a different team from the one that attacks it.
- **Scope.** Only the target team's **staging** environment and its public interfaces: chat, APIs, documents that users can upload, and MCP endpoints exposed to clients. Out of scope:
  - Azure control-plane attacks;
  - denial of service beyond a declared request-rate ceiling;
  - social engineering of people;
  - anything touching other teams or the course gateway.
- **Credentials.** Attackers get standard user accounts for each role defined by the target team. The target team must provide these.
- **Findings.** Each finding is filed as an issue in the target repo using the template: title, OWASP/ASI mapping, reproduction, impact, ASR where measurable, suggested mitigation. Severity is agreed between the teams or decided by the instructor.
- **Defence.** The target team triages within 24 h and either fixes the finding with a regression eval or accepts the risk with an owner and rationale signed off by the "client". An unresolved critical finding at PRR blocks a pass.
- **Ethics.** All data is synthetic, but behave as if it were real: no exfiltration beyond proof, and no persistence mechanisms left behind.

## 6. Production-readiness review (PRR) checklist

The PRR panel works through this list. Each item is pass, conditional or fail, and conditional items must be closed before M6.

1. **Reproducible deploy.** Clean-subscription deploy completes in a supervised run.
2. **Eval gates.** Blocking in CI, pass on `main`, nightly uncached run green or triaged, and the gate-sensitivity check passed. Instructors mutate the system and the team's gate must catch the change.
3. **Hidden acceptance suite.** At or above the brief's thresholds per stratum, with safety items at pass^3.
4. **Load test at peak profile.** p95 within NFR, graceful degradation demonstrated, and 429/failover behaviour verified.
5. **Cost.** Actuals within ±25% of the cost model at the tested load. A deviation is acceptable if it is explained and the model is corrected.
6. **DR drill.** Agent layer and index reconstructed from code within the stated RTO, with timings recorded.
7. **Model migration rehearsal.** One model role swapped via config. Pairwise per-failure-mode evals and a go/no-go note.
8. **Security.** Threat model current, red-team findings resolved or accepted, no high or critical CodeQL alerts, secrets scanning clean, agent identities least-privilege.
9. **Privacy.** Content capture off by default (or justified with a DPIA reference), retention implemented, redaction tests pass (Briefs A and B).
10. **AI Act risk file.** Complete, consistent with the implemented system, and the prohibited-features list enforced by tests.
11. **Runbook.** Each alert links to a runbook entry, and one entry is executed live by a team member chosen by the panel.
12. **Factory.** Metrics page live, branch protection on, and the human-review requirement enforced.

## 7. Assessment

### 7.1 Weights

| Component | Weight | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Team grade** | 60% | Milestones M1–M6 (weights in §5), scored with the team rubric (§7.3) |
| **Individual grade** | 40% | Individual oral (40% of individual) + contribution evidence (30%) + track-specific deliverable review (30%) |

**Peer assessment** adjusts the *individual* grade within ±10%. Each member distributes points across teammates per criterion (contribution, reliability, quality, collaboration), WebPA-style. Instructors moderate outliers and patterns of collusion. Peer scores never move a learner across the pass/fail line on their own. A move across that line requires corroborating evidence from Git, the session logs or mentor notes.

### 7.2 Hard requirements (both team and individual)

**Team:**
- The system is deployed and the **hidden acceptance suite** runs against it.
- All CI gates are enforced.
- No unresolved critical red-team finding.
- The AI Act risk file exists with a classification rationale.
- The PRR is passed or conditional, with conditions closed.

**Individual:**
- Merged PR count and ADR authorship are met.
- The **individual oral is passed** under the ownership rule: each learner must explain and modify their own components (see [Assessment and gates](start-here/assessment-gates.md) §2).
- No integrity breach.

### 7.3 Team rubric

| Criterion | Novice | Competent | Proficient | Expert |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem & value framing** | Builds what was asked literally | Scoped use cases with NFRs signed off | Use cases chosen and sized by value and risk; hidden complexities elicited; explicit non-goals | Reframes the client's ask to more value or less risk, backed by a convincing business case |
| **System quality** | Demo works on the happy path | Hidden acceptance suite at threshold | Above threshold in all strata incl. non-EN languages; safety items pass^3 | Robust under the board's live chaos action; graceful degradation shown |
| **Verification** | Generic metrics | Failure-driven evals gate CI | Validated judges, per-language results, red-team ASR reduced by architectural fixes, gate-sensitivity passed | Evals become a product asset: the client could run them on a vendor change |
| **Operability** | No runbook | Dashboards, alerts and runbook exist | Cost model matches actuals within tolerance; DR drill within RTO; migration rehearsal done | Operations would survive the team's departure: runbooks executed by someone else succeed |
| **Security & governance** | Classifier-only defences; AI Act section boilerplate | Threat model and risk file present and consistent | Trifecta legs removed by design; classification argued per function; oversight designed to work | Governance evidence generated by the pipeline (eval reports, scorecards, logs) and traceable to obligations |
| **Architecture decisions & communication** | Decisions undocumented | ADRs with options and consequences | ADRs linked to evidence; C4 accurate; trade-offs explained to non-technical stakeholders | Simplest design that meets NFRs, with credible review-by triggers |
| **Factory & delivery** | Agents used ad hoc | Gates, review and memory files in place | Metrics show healthy flow (first-pass acceptance, rework, cost per PR) and are used to adjust practice | Factory assets reusable by another team; governance policy explicit |

### 7.4 Track-specific individual review

**Developer track** (reviewed by an instructor against the code-review rubric) covers:
- correctness and test or eval coverage of the learner's owned components;
- security;
- observability;
- cost per task;
- quality of the eval suite and judges authored;
- one deep-dive: a PR the learner chooses and walks through, including what the agent got wrong.

**Architect track** (reviewed by the board architect) covers:
- the ADRs authored;
- the learner's sections of the WAF AI assessment, cost model, DR plan or AI Act risk file;
- the stakeholder pitch;
- one design scenario answered live: "the client's budget halves", "a second tenant joins", or "the regulator asks for your logs".

## 8. Final defense and review board

**Board:**
- **Chair:** course lead.
- **External practitioner:** a senior architect or AI engineer from industry, briefed on the rubric.
- **Security and RAI reviewer.**
- **The client:** the instructor who played the client throughout.
- **Optional:** a member of the paired red-team team (non-voting, asks one question).

**Format (≈ 100 min per team):**

| Time | Segment |
|---|---|
| 15 min | **Client pitch**: business outcome, what was built, what wasn't and why, costs, risks. Delivered to the client in non-technical language. |
| 20 min | **Live demo**: the board triggers a fresh hidden acceptance-suite run and one **chaos action** chosen on the day (kill a container, revoke an agent identity's role, inject a poisoned document, exhaust a quota, flip a model deployment). The team narrates detection and recovery. |
| 35 min | **Architecture and operations questioning** across the package: ADR challenges, cost model, DR, threat model, AI Act classification |
| 25 min | **Individual questioning**: ~5 min per learner on their own components and decisions, under the ownership rule |
| 5 min | Close; the team states the top 3 things they would do next |

**Decision protocol:**
1. Each board member scores independently against the rubric before discussion.
2. The chair runs a calibration discussion for any criterion where scores differ by ≥ 2 levels.
3. Possible outcomes:
   - **Pass** (with level per criterion).
   - **Pass with conditions**: specific fixes within 2 weeks, verified by a mentor.
   - **Not yet**: the team or the individual rejoins the next cohort's capstone. Individuals may join another team as a sixth member if needed.
4. Written feedback within 5 working days.

**Peer review of 3 teams** (week 7, the LLM Zoomcamp pattern): each learner reviews 3 other teams' PRR packages using the PRR checklist and writes ≥ 3 specific, actionable findings per team. Review quality is graded as part of contribution evidence. Useful reviews cite evidence and propose a fix.

## 9. Environment and budget

- **One Azure subscription per team.** It is created under the course management group with policies for allowed EU regions, denied expensive SKUs unless approved (PTU, Firewall Premium, GPU, DDoS Network Protection), required tags and diagnostic caps.
- **Indicative budget cap:** €600 per team for 8 weeks (course-designer default, within the €300–1,200 range estimated in the README; recalibrate per release with the pricing calculator). Alerts go to the team and a mentor at 50% and 80% of actual spend and 100% of forecast. At 100% of actual, non-production resources are stopped automatically.
- **Model access** goes through the course AI gateway, with a per-team key and token quota. Teams may add their own Foundry deployments for evaluation of alternatives within budget.
- **Private networking** (the baseline reference architecture's Firewall, App Gateway WAF and private endpoints) is required **only during the PRR window** for teams whose NFRs demand it, deployed from IaC and torn down afterwards. Otherwise its design is documented and reviewed but not left running.

## 10. Portfolio guidance

The capstone is the strongest artefact you will have at the end of the course. Present it so a hiring manager or client can verify your claims.

**Publish (after the final defense, with team agreement):**
- A **curated public repository**, or a public copy of the team repo, containing synthetic data only. Before publishing, rewrite history or run gitleaks over the full history, and remove gateway keys, subscription ids and tenant ids.
- A **case-study README**: client problem (fictional), constraints, architecture (C4 diagram), the 3–5 key decisions with the evidence that drove them, results (hidden acceptance-suite numbers per stratum, ASR before and after, cost per task vs target, DR drill timing), what you would do differently, and **your own contribution stated precisely**.
- A **3–5 minute demo video** that includes one failure and its handling, not only the happy path.
- 2–3 of your ADRs, an excerpt of the eval report, and the red-team postmortem.
- A competency statement per the [Competency matrix](start-here/competency-matrix.md) §5 with evidence links.

**Do not publish:**
- hidden acceptance-suite cases or results per case;
- other teams' findings about your system without their consent, or your findings about theirs;
- the client role-play transcripts;
- anything that implies the fictional organisations are real or that the system ran in production with real users.

**Licensing:** default to MIT for team code with all members listed, and CC BY 4.0 for documentation, unless the team agrees otherwise in `TEAM.md`. Course-provided starter code and data keep their own licences.

**Honest framing:** "Built and defended a production-grade, evaluated multi-agent system for a simulated municipal client in an 8-week team capstone" is accurate and strong. "Deployed AI for a municipality" is not accurate.
